# French (`fr`)

Write in contemporary standard French in Latin script. Since `fr` is not tied
to a country, select forms intelligible across the Francophone world and avoid
unnecessary France-only colloquialisms. Use a sober lexicographic register,
not archaic scriptural French or the language of a particular published
translation.

Concept labels should be concise noun phrases, adjective phrases, or
infinitives. Supply an article only when French requires it for a natural,
unambiguous label; avoid turning every gloss into a full clause. Preserve
grammatical gender where it bears on reference, pronominal verbs where `se`
changes the sense, and required prepositions such as `à` or `de`. Distinguish
an action from its result, a quality from its bearer, and an inchoative from a
causative. Contextual glosses may spell out the object or relation when a bare
French word would be misleading. Avoid ambiguous stacked `de` phrases and
overuse of abstract nouns in `-tion`. Lexical-unit glosses must preserve the
attested category, argument frame, and construction-bound restriction rather
than generalize a contextual use.

Prefer established French equivalents to raw Arabic transliterations.
Arabic-derived Islamic terms can be retained only when conventional in French
and semantically exact; many circulate with specialized, political, or
confessional senses that do not match a lexical branch. Avoid source-shaped
calques and false friends carried through international vocabulary. Arabic
evidence must not leak into the target as unexplained Arabic wording.
Independently formulate the French: never reproduce Turkish word order,
possessive chains, light-verb phrases, or productive nominalizations
mechanically.

Use conventional French names for well-established scriptural and historical
figures. For other names, adopt a consistent Francophone transliteration,
without Arabic inflection or gratuitous diacritics that impede recognition.
Do not replace a proper name with its etymology unless lexical wordplay is part
of the represented sense.

Review agreement, gender, article choice, reflexivity, valency, and the scope
of negation or evaluation. Test whether apparently elegant words add Catholic,
legal, colonial, or modern political connotations. Check especially broad
terms such as `foi`, `charité`, `piété`, and `tentation` against the actual
facets. The final wording should sound natively French, remain compact, and
not be Turkish phrased in French vocabulary.
