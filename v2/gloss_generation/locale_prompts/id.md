# Indonesian (`id`)

Write in contemporary Standard Indonesian using Latin script and current
Indonesian spelling. Prefer neutral, widely understood formal vocabulary over
regional colloquialisms, unnecessarily literary Malay, or constructions that
sound Malaysian rather than Indonesian.

Use compact noun or adjective phrases and natural verbal labels. Choose
Indonesian derivation deliberately: `meN-`, `ber-`, `ter-`, `di-`, `-kan`,
`-i`, `peN-`, and `ke-…-an` can change voice, valency, affectedness, agency,
or state. Do not strip an affix merely to obtain a short root if the resulting
form has a different category or is not independently idiomatic. Preserve the
difference between process and result, accidental `ter-` and stative `ter-`,
agent and instrument, and transitive and intransitive readings. Contextual
glosses may include a complement or participant when an unaffixed base is too
polysemous. Use reduplication only when plurality, iteration, or
lexicalization requires it.

Lexical-unit glosses must preserve the attested affixation, category, valency,
and construction-bound scope rather than elevate a contextual use.

Arabic loans are extensive in Indonesian Islamic vocabulary, but familiar
forms may have local ritual, institutional, or moral meanings different from
the Arabic source. Use established Indonesian spelling and test the
Indonesian sense; raw scholarly transliteration is not an Indonesian gloss.
Avoid hyper-Arabic spelling and pseudo-Arabic calques. Dutch, English, and
Sanskrit loans can also introduce modern or nonreligious senses. Do not
mechanically copy Turkish word order, possessive chains, light verbs, or
suffix-derived abstractions, and do not route the wording through Malaysian
Malay.

Use conventional Indonesian forms for familiar proper names. Less familiar
Arabic names should follow consistent Indonesian spelling, without source
case endings, gratuitous apostrophes, or added honorifics. Do not translate a
name’s etymology unless wordplay is explicitly active.

Review affix choice, nasal assimilation, voice, valency, reduplication, and
whether a root form is actually acceptable as a label. Check Arabic-derived
terms for Indonesian semantic drift and unwanted institutional overtones.
Confirm natural Indonesian collocation, concise lexicographic form, preserved
participants and facets, and no Malaysian, Arabic-source, or Turkish-shaped
leakage.
