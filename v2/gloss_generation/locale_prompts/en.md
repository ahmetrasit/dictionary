# English (`en`)

Write compact glosses in clear, contemporary international English. Because
`en` has no region subtag, prefer wording shared across major standards; avoid
US- or UK-specific senses when a neutral alternative exists. Use ordinary
English spelling consistently within a candidate, and do not force archaic or
biblical diction merely because the source is Qurʾanic.

Concept glosses should normally be short noun phrases, adjective phrases, or
bare infinitival verb phrases: “deliberate concealment,” “steadfast,” “to
reconcile.” Contextual glosses may be slightly more explicit when an English
headword would otherwise hide an argument, participant, or evaluative facet.
Lexical glosses must preserve whether the source unit names an act, state,
agent, patient, quality, or relation. English articles are usually omitted in
dictionary labels unless definiteness is part of the sense. Preserve
count/mass distinctions, transitivity, reflexivity, and active versus passive
orientation. Do not turn a causative into a simple intransitive, or a relational
adjective into an abstract noun for elegance.

Prefer established plain-English terminology over opaque transliteration.
Arabic-derived terms already naturalized in English may be used only when
their current English sense fits the represented concept; flag familiar terms
whose religious, legal, or popular meanings are broader or narrower. Avoid
unexplained Arabic plurals and pseudo-technical calques such as unnatural
“people of X” constructions. Arabic evidence is semantic evidence, not text to
copy. Likewise, never mechanically translate Turkish word order, suffix
structure, light-verb constructions, or conventional religious phrasing.

Render widely established proper names in their standard English forms. For
less established names, use a consistent readable scholarly transliteration
without importing case endings or honorifics. Do not translate a proper name
as though it were a descriptive common noun unless the context genuinely
activates that lexical meaning.

Review for false synonymy, excessive abstraction, unwanted moral or doctrinal
overtones, and words whose everyday English sense has drifted (“charity,”
“temptation,” “unbelief,” and similar high-risk vocabulary). Check that every
phrase sounds like an English dictionary gloss, retains the source’s semantic
center and participant roles, and does not read like Turkish in English words.
