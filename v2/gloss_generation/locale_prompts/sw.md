# Swahili (`sw`)

Write contemporary Standard Swahili (Kiswahili sanifu) in Latin script.
Prefer vocabulary widely accepted across East African standard usage and
avoid strongly local dialect forms, country-specific slang, or needlessly
archaic coastal diction.

Use compact noun or adjective phrases and natural verbal labels. Preserve
noun-class membership and concord where an agreeing word is present; a class
shift can change animacy, collectivity, size, or evaluative meaning. In verbal
forms, distinguish active, passive, stative, causative, applicative,
reciprocal, and reflexive extensions, and preserve participant roles and
valency. A bare infinitival `ku-` form may be suitable for a verbal concept,
but do not add tense, subject, or object markers that turn a label into an
unnecessary finite clause. Contextual glosses may state an object or relation
when the short verb is too broad. Avoid opaque strings of noun-class-derived
abstracts.

Lexical-unit glosses must preserve noun class or verbal extension, argument
frame, and construction-bound scope rather than promote a local use.

Arabic loans are an integral historical layer of Swahili, especially in
religious vocabulary, but they have undergone Swahili phonological,
morphological, and semantic adaptation. Use the established Swahili form and
meaning; do not insert Arabic script, scholarly transliteration, or an
unfamiliar cognate merely because it resembles the evidence. Some familiar
loans have specialized coastal, Islamic, legal, or modern political senses.
English loans may likewise broaden the register. Never mechanically copy
Turkish word order, compounds, light verbs, or suffix patterns.

Use conventional Swahili forms for familiar scriptural and historical proper
names. Render less familiar Arabic names consistently in Swahili spelling,
without source case endings or unrequested honorifics. Do not replace a proper
name with its etymology unless contextual wordplay is explicit.

Review noun class, agreement, singular/plural pairing, verbal extensions,
valency, and standard East African naturalness. Check Arabic-derived terms for
Swahili semantic drift and distinguish legitimate loans from copied source
material. Reject Arabic-shaped, English-shaped, or Turkish-shaped calques.
Confirm a concise, idiomatic Standard Swahili gloss with the intended semantic
head, scope, and participant roles.
